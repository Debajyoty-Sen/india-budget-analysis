A Decade of Fiscal Expansion
India's Union Budget more than doubled in a decade — from ₹21.5 lakh crore in 2017-18 to ₹53.5 lakh crore in 2026-27, a 149% increase. This section establishes the macro context before examining where the money actually went.

Key Observation
Growth slowed to 9.2% in 2020-21 — the only year below 10% in the entire decade — directly attributable to COVID-19 fiscal conservatism. Post-2023, growth has moderated again as base effects kick in and consolidation pressures mount. The structural upward trajectory, however, is unbroken.

Welfare vs. Infrastructure

The Crossover — When Infrastructure Overtook Welfare
In 2017-18, social sector commanded 11.2% of the budget vs infrastructure's 6.9%. By 2026-27, these trajectories have completely crossed: infrastructure now commands 12.1% while social sector has contracted to 8.5%. The state is explicitly prioritising physical capital formation over direct human capital transfers — a "build the highway, let growth follow" philosophy.

Policy Implication
The trajectories crossed in 2022-23. This is not accidental — it reflects a deliberate post-COVID fiscal philosophy where infrastructure-led job creation (multiplier effects) is preferred over direct welfare expansion. The bet is that roads and railways generate more durable income than subsidies.

The Counterintuitive Finding — Space
Despite high-profile successes like Chandrayaan-3 and Aditya-L1, the Department of Space grew at only 4.6% CAGR — less than half the budget average. This is not neglect but strategy: the government is deliberately transitioning space from a state-led model to a private-sector-led enterprise via IN-SPACe, rather than expanding ISRO's state budget.

Policy Implication
The "crowding in" hypothesis applies specifically to transport connectivity — not to broad-based industrialisation. The state is building the physical nervous system of the economy (roads, rail corridors), betting the private sector will build factories and businesses along those arteries. This is a coherent but concentrated strategy, not a systemic capex push.

Verdict: Partial Structural Shift
COVID caused a selective structural shift. Health spending permanently re-rated: pre-COVID average was ₹56K Cr, post-COVID average is ₹95K Cr — it never reverted. Rural Development, which surged for MGNREGA during the lockdown, continued its gradual upward trend but without retaining the COVID-era spike proportionally. No ministry reverted to pre-2020 absolute levels — the fiscal floor was raised permanently.

Policy Implication
The macro Capex story masks a deeply uneven picture at the ministry level. Defence shows genuine — if slow — improvement: its Rev:Cap ratio fell from 2.93 to 2.40 as military modernisation spending increased. Home Affairs is worsening: bloat peaked at 10.30 in 2024-25, meaning ₹10 in paramilitary salaries and operational costs for every ₹1 in police infrastructure. Agriculture is almost entirely Revenue-driven — PM-KISAN, fertiliser subsidies, and MSP operations leave almost no room for capital investment in the sector.

Note on Agriculture & Education
Extremely high Revenue:Capital ratios in Agriculture and Education do not necessarily indicate mismanagement. Agriculture Capex is near-zero because PM-KISAN is a direct income transfer (Revenue). Education Capex is low because school/university construction is largely a state government responsibility — the Centre funds via grants (Revenue). Context matters when interpreting this ratio.
